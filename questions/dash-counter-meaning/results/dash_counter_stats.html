The Shared Counter — ACP-127 Tape Relay Stats

Data Notes · ACP-127 Corpus, Full Extraction

One counter, one relay, a ledger far bigger than our archive

The dash-counter line's two digit groups turn out to be ACP-127's own "station serial number" and "filing time" — a single shared tape-relay sequence, not a per-cable ID. Comparing it against what actually survives in this corpus implies most of what it counted never made it into the declassified record — and a second, independent signal (which cables have real body text at all) lines up with a published study's own findings on where and when text went missing.

Summed across every day with counter data, the counter implies at least 243,235,084 messages passed through this relay system — against 1,827,495 cables actually archived here. The ratio holds remarkably steady, ~105–165×, for six straight years.

First, where this line actually lives

An unlabeled stamp between the signoff block and the DTG

The dash-counter line has no caption of its own in ACP-127 — it isn't one of the protocol's formally named fields. It sits, unlabeled, between the drafting/clearance signoff and the date-time group. Real example (1977STATE013174):

DRAFTED BY EB/OA/AVP:JSGRAVATT:FAA:CSPICKENS:JO APPROVED BY EB/OA/AVP:AJWHITE NEA/ARN - MLKING ------------------201212Z 109980 /20 R 192358Z JAN 77 FM SECSTATE WASHDC TO AMEMBASSY TRIPOLI

PAGE line → ORIGIN/INFO distribution → drafter/clearance signoff → dash-counter line → DTG → FM/TO. Highlighted: filing_time=201212Z (the 20th at 1212Z), counter=109980, copies=20. Nothing in ACP-127(G) documents a line in this position — it reads as the tape-relay communications centre's own handling stamp, not something the drafting officer composed, which is exactly what the "single shared sequence" finding below confirms.

One shared counter, not per-embassy

Which station increments the counter, and what else confirms it

Station attribution and cross-checks against other signals

Sorting every counter-bearing document by (date, station) shows, for the subset of counter values with a surviving document, which station's traffic occupies which part of the sequence — the counter's implied volume runs ~100× larger than this archive, so most ticks have no attributable station here, but the ticks we do have show clear structure, cross-checked against two other independent fields.

Counter order tracks filing_time, not DTG

Spearman correlation between counter value and reconstructed filing_time, per DTG-day: raw result was noisy, but every one of the 15 worst-correlated days turned out to have a counter reset occurring mid-day — a confound, not disorder. Restricted to 145 clean (no mid-day reset) days:

Counter vs. filing_time (median ρ)
0.96
Counter vs. DTG / origination time (median ρ)
0.73

Zero of the 145 clean days show a negative correlation with filing_time. The weaker DTG relationship is exactly what's expected if the counter stamps arrival at the relay (filing_time), not when the cable was drafted (DTG).

Overseas posts batch; STATE is continuous

STATE alone accounts for 28.7% of all counter-bearing records (524,763 of 1,826,811) — vastly more than any single overseas post (next highest, Geneva, at 2.0%). More telling is how each station's values are distributed across a day: comparing a station's actual average gap between consecutive counter values to what a uniform random draw of the same size would produce across that day's full range.

Station typeMedian clustering ratioReading
Overseas posts0.16tightly clustered — a day's cables land in one contiguous burst (many posts <0.02)
STATE≈1.0 (0.994–1.024)indistinguishable from uniform random — spread continuously across the whole day

Concrete example, 1978-10-23: Tokyo's 29 cables that day span counters 59,374–61,007 (a 1,633-wide band) inside a day-wide range of 25,468–111,340 — under 2% of the day's spread. STATE's cables that same day ran continuously from 25,468 past 63,000. Consistent with overseas posts' traffic arriving in scheduled daily transmission sessions, while STATE's own traffic originates continuously on-site at the relay.

Cross-check: Document Number's per-station serial (the "MRN")

The numeric suffix of Document Number (e.g. 013174 in 1977STATE013174) is a separate signal: a per-station, per-year sequential cable number, unrelated to the ACP-127 counter itself. It correlates with the dash-counter station-day by station-day on clean days — median ρ=0.80 across all stations, ρ=0.91 for STATE specifically — independent confirmation of the filing_time result above, from a completely different field.

It also gives an independent, verifiable measure of real cable volume. Taking the robust 99.9th-percentile serial per year for STATE (excluding a small tail of placeholder-like values ≥900,000, 0–67 docs/year):

Whatever the counter tallies, it isn't explained by State Department cable traffic alone; that traffic's real volume, measured directly, is two orders of magnitude too small.

Signals checked and ruled out

Three more metadata fields were checked as possible counter-related signals; none showed a connection:

  • Film Number (e.g. D760210-0639, a microfilm reel + frame ID): one reel spans 8 distinct draft dates and 142 distinct stations, with frame numbers topping out around 1,200–2,500 — two orders of magnitude below the counter's ~130,000 ceiling. A NARA microfilming batch, unrelated to transmission order.
  • Legacy Key (e.g. link1973/newtext/t19730629/aaaajogf.tel): the batch segment spans up to 27 distinct draft dates; the filename suffix increments as pure scan order — within one station's cluster, Document Numbers actually run backward, confirming archival scanning order, not transmission order.
  • Control Number: present on only 783 of 2,081,272 records, and groups multiple distinct cables under one ID — an administrative case number, not a per-message counter.

A second, independent signal

Where the message text itself goes missing

Separate from the counter: every cable record also carries a classification level and either real body text or a placeholder (ERROR READING TEXT INDEX / EXPAND ERROR ENCOUNTERED) where the text should be. Souza, Coelho, Shah & Connelly's 2016 study "Using Artificial Intelligence to Identify State Secrets" (arXiv:1611.00356), working from the same NARA-derived cable population, found specific date ranges where this placeholder rate spikes — interpreted as cables likely destroyed or removed after transmission but before declassification review. Their study covers 1973–1978; this corpus runs one year further, into 1979.

The counter-implied total is a lower bound (per-day min/max span, see caveats) and still runs 55–76× the FOIA-reported system-wide SAS total. What that gap consists of isn't established here — the counter is a relay-centre transmission log (see "where the counter sits in the raw header" above) and nothing in this repository's data says what else, if anything, shared that relay with State Department traffic. What Souza et al. (2016) do document, from NARA's own appraisal records (pp. 7–8), is that the cable population behind both this corpus and their study is already a curated subset of something much larger: 27 million CFPF electronic records had accumulated by 2006, and archivists deliberately preserved only cables with historically-significant TAGS, explicitly reducing the retained share of routine, unclassified cables. That figure spans decades of CFPF record-keeping generally, not just 1973–1979, so it can't be turned into a precise ratio here — but it shows substantial, documented filtering happened well before the counter's implied volume enters the comparison. FOIA/SAS and our corpus, by contrast, stay within the same order of magnitude of each other throughout (both are specifically State Department cable counts), which is why that pair gets its own ratio column in the table above rather than needing a log axis here.

Strong confirmation of all four — but with a nuance the paper's prose blurs: it describes these as periods with "almost no cables," implying absence. Our data shows the opposite mechanism — metadata records are present at normal volume (1,000–1,600/day); what's missing is specifically body text. That's the paper's own "error messages for body" category, not a document-count gap. We also found "May 25–31" and "June 1976" aren't two separate events — missing % never drops below 94% across the full six weeks from May 25 to July 2. Same for the other two: measured directly rather than read off a chart, Dec 1975's gap runs Nov 26–Dec 24, and March 1976's runs Mar 17–Apr 5 — both wider than their named windows.

New: three of the paper's own unlabeled spikes, precisely dated

The paper's Fig. 2 chart plots missing-cable counts through 1973–1978 and visibly shows six spikes, but only names four in its text. Scanning our own data the same way (raw daily missing count, not just percentage, matching that chart's y-axis) turns up three ranges whose magnitude lines up with the paper's own unlabeled peaks almost exactly — giving them real dates for the first time:

RangePeak dayPeak missingPaper's Fig. 2 estimate

The paper offers no historical explanation for these three, and none is asserted here either — they're reported purely as precise dates for spikes that were already visible, but unlabeled, in the paper's own figure. A smaller, borderline cluster also appears Oct 18–23, 1973 (peak 245/day), below the magnitude of the other spikes.

Outside the paper's coverage: Oct 30–31, 1979

Small in absolute terms (93 missing at peak) but sharply anomalous against 1979's otherwise near-zero baseline (median 6/day, max elsewhere 33). The timing coincides with the intensifying Iran crisis — the Shah was admitted to the US for medical treatment Oct 22, 1979; the US embassy in Tehran was seized Nov 4, 1979. Noted as a temporal coincidence only; nothing here demonstrates a causal link.

1977 and 1978 show almost no gap activity at all — median 0 and 3 missing/day respectively, max 4 and 13 — a strong independent confirmation of the paper's own claim that "the gaps end with the end of Kissinger's term as Secretary of State" (Jan 20, 1977).

Caveats

implied_volume_naive_span (max counter − min counter + 1, per day) is a lower bound, not a precise reconstruction — it can't detect a reset happening mid-day. A more ambitious attempt to walk every record in reconstructed filing-time order across the whole corpus, resolving resets explicitly, produced internally inconsistent results (~42% of adjacent pairs showed backward steps even after reconstruction — too high for noise), suggesting multiple interleaved sub-sequences (parallel precedence-tier queues, multiple relay legs) that a single global ordering can't cleanly capture. The per-day span sidesteps that problem entirely, at the cost of being a floor rather than an exact count.

The classification/availability cross-check trusts Original Classification and the placeholder-text pattern as extracted from source plaintext — original SAS-database text with noise added by NARA's own reproduction process, not OCR. A ~185-document residue of garbled classification values across the full corpus is bucketed separately and excluded from the cross-check tables.

Pre-1976 documents have counter but no filing_time — the day-span comparison still works for those years (needs only counter). The narrower filing_time-vs-DTG lag analysis (1976–1979 only) has moved to its own note, dash_counter_filing_lag.md, since it's a secondary finding distinct from the core counter/gap story here.
